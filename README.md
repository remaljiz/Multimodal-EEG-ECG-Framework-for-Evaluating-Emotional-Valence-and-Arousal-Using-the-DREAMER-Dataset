# Multimodal EEG ECG Framework for Evaluating Emotional Valence and Arousal Using the DREAMER Dataset
This project develops a multimodal EEG–ECG framework for evaluating emotional valence and arousal using the DREAMER dataset. Grounded in Russell’s Circumplex Model of Affect, the project conceptualizes emotion along two major dimensions: valence, which represents the pleasantness or negativity of an emotional state, and arousal, which represents the intensity of physiological activation. To make these dimensions observable, the study compares two contrasting audio-visual emotional conditions: calm, characterized by high valence and low arousal, and fear, characterized by low valence and high arousal.

The framework integrates central nervous system activity from EEG and autonomic nervous system activity from ECG. For EEG analysis, emotional valence is estimated using Frontal Alpha Asymmetry, calculated from alpha-band activity in the F3 and F4 frontal channels. Based on the approach-withdrawal model, relatively stronger left-frontal activation is interpreted as positive emotional processing, while stronger right-frontal activation is associated with negative affect. For ECG analysis, autonomic arousal is evaluated through heart rate variability features, including RMSSD and the LF/HF ratio. RMSSD reflects parasympathetic regulation, whereas LF/HF provides an index of sympathovagal balance.

A NeuroPype-based pipeline is designed to process synchronized EEG and ECG recordings. The workflow includes file import, stream separation, timestamp correction, channel localization, filtering, artifact removal, rereferencing, spectral analysis, R-peak detection, and feature extraction. The project also includes visual inspection of artifacts such as eye blinks, baseline drift, sweat artifacts, and possible electrode disconnection, ensuring that preprocessing decisions are physiologically meaningful.

The results show that calm and fear conditions produce distinguishable multimodal patterns. EEG alpha asymmetry generally indicates higher valence during calm stimuli, while ECG LF/HF ratios tend to be higher during fear stimuli, suggesting stronger autonomic arousal. Moving-window analysis further reveals participant-specific temporal dynamics, such as gradual emotional accumulation or rapid habituation. Although the analysis is limited to two participants and the final 60 seconds of each stimulus, the project demonstrates how EEG and ECG can be combined to characterize emotional responses and support future applications in affective computing, emotion recognition, and human-computer interaction. Overall, it bridges theoretical emotion models with practical biosignal processing strategies and interpretable physiological feature extraction clearly.
